## Version B — fuller

**Subject:** Alumni feedback — grant and agreement administration in the EM curriculum

Dear Professor [Name],

I hope this finds you well. I completed the BAS in Emergency Management at Pierce,
and I'm writing with a piece of alumni feedback I think is worth putting in front of
the program.

Since becoming more involved with the City of Everett's emergency management work,
I've noticed one area I don't remember encountering directly at either Edmonds or
Pierce: **grant and agreement administration as it functions in local emergency
management.** I've volunteered with the City for several years, and lately I've been
working DR-4906 as an applicant recovering disaster-related expenditures. I was
cross-trained in Public Assistance during my time at FEMA and worked in both NEMIS
and EMMIE, so I came in with more background than most. Those platforms are
FEMA-specific, but the underlying dynamics between federal, state, and local
government funding are general — and they're what local emergency managers live in.

### What the work actually consists of

Programs like EMPG are central to sustaining local emergency management capability,
and a great deal of the job is administrative in a specific, learnable way:
interpreting grant requirements, developing work plans and budgets, documenting
match, submitting reimbursement requests, following procurement rules, drafting
scopes of work, working with RFPs, establishing MOUs and interlocal agreements, and
maintaining documentation for reporting and closeout.

Public Assistance makes this concrete. Sitting through the Recovery Scoping Meeting
for DR-4906 last month, what struck me was how much of the process is knowable in
advance — the phases, the deadlines, the documentation, the decision points — and how
much of it a new emergency manager would have to learn by getting it wrong first.

### The specific things I'd want a student to leave knowing

- **The four-part eligibility test.** FEMA tests applicant, facility, work, and cost
  independently, and a failure on any one ends the project regardless of the other
  three. That framing alone reorganizes how you read a damage inventory.
- **Small versus large projects.** The thresholds ($4,100 minimum, $1,093,800 for
  large-project treatment) decide whether a project is written and paid on an
  estimate or on actual cost with 10% retainage, progressive draws, a final
  inspection, and a different closeout document. Two projects $100,000 apart in cost
  are administered completely differently for the following two years.
- **The 75/25 cost share**, and the fact that the local 25% is real money that has to
  come from a general fund, a utility fund, or donated resources credited against it.
- **The work categories**, because they change what is reimbursable:
  - Emergency work — **Cat-A** debris removal and **Cat-B** emergency protective
    measures. Cat-A reimburses all force account labor; Cat-B reimburses budgeted
    employees for *overtime only*. That one asymmetry is, in my experience, the single
    most common and expensive misunderstanding an applicant has.
  - Permanent work — **Cat-C through G**, which is also where Section 406 hazard
    mitigation becomes available to protect the damaged element against the next event.
  - **Cat-I** for building code and floodplain management enforcement, on a 180-day
    deadline FEMA will not extend.
  - **Cat-Z** for grant management costs, capped at 5% of the applicant's total
    obligated amount — money applicants routinely leave on the table because nobody
    tracked staff time from the start of the grant.
- **Procurement**, which is where otherwise-eligible work gets de-obligated later.
  Micro-purchase and simplified acquisition thresholds, SAM.gov debarment checks, and
  the fact that cost-plus-percentage-of-cost contracts are prohibited outright and
  are often buried in language that's hard to spot.

### What I'd suggest

I'm not suggesting an entirely new course. This could fit as a module, a workshop, or
an applied assignment within something that already exists. Students could review a
simplified grant agreement, develop a small work plan and budget, draft an MOU or
scope of work, interpret an RFP, or assemble a mock reimbursement package as a
post-disaster applicant.

Looking back, the closest courses at Pierce were most likely EM 310, EM 430, EM 480,
and the project management sequence. The PM program leaned heavily on PMBOK/PMP
practice — a useful foundation, but it doesn't reach the EM-specific administrative
work. I also completed technical writing at Edmonds, which satisfied Pierce's
technical-writing requirement. All of those were valuable. I just don't remember an
assignment in which we interpreted or produced these kinds of emergency management
documents.

### An offer

I only recognized this as a potential gap after seeing what local emergency managers
encounter routinely, and I thought it was worth sharing as alumni feedback rather
than criticism — the program prepared me well for a great deal else.

I'd be glad to help. Specifically, I've built a working simulator of the Public
Assistance reimbursement process that could serve directly as the applied assignment
I'm describing. It walks a student through the whole applicant side: entering a
damage inventory, grouping sites into projects, building costs against the actual
FEMA equipment rate schedule, and assembling a submission package. It then reviews
the package the way a FEMA reviewer would and scores it, with every finding cited to
the governing policy. It ships with a fictional disaster scenario seeded with
realistic errors, so a student starts with a failing package and works it clean.

It runs in a browser and requires no installation on a student's part. I'd be happy
to demonstrate it, hand it over for the program to use and modify, or help outline
the exercise around it — whichever is most useful. It's also built to be
disaster-agnostic, so if it's ever useful to other jurisdictions in the region going
through what Everett has, it would work for them too.

Thank you for your time, and for the program.

Best regards,
Joshua D. Curry

## Notes before you send

- **Pick your recipients deliberately.** A department chair or program coordinator can
  act on this; a single instructor can champion it. Consider sending to the Pierce EM
  program lead first and copying anyone you had a good relationship with.
- **Verify the thresholds before you send.** The $4,100 and $1,093,800 figures are
  from the DR-4906 Recovery Scoping Meeting materials and are indexed annually. If
  you'd rather not date the email, say "currently around $4,100 and roughly $1.1
  million."
- **The course numbers are your recollection.** If you can check a catalog quickly it
  strengthens the message; if not, "the closest fits were probably…" is honest and
  fine as written.
- **Don't attach the DR-4906 documents.** They contain City staff names, direct phone
  numbers, and email addresses. If you want to show something concrete, the simulator
  with the fictional Cascade Valley scenario makes the same point and carries no
  disclosure risk.
- **Version A is likelier to get read and answered.** Version B is better if you're
  sending to someone who already knows you, or as a follow-up once someone bites.
